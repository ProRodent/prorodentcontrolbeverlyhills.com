Scratching Noises in the Attic at Night: Mice or Rats?

The timing, weight and pattern of the noise you hear overhead are better clues than guessing based on how the house looks from outside.

Why nighttime specifically.

Mice and rats are both nocturnal, most active from dusk until a few hours before dawn, which is exactly the window when a quiet house makes even a small sound overhead easy to notice. During the day, both species typically rest in a nest and produce little or no noise, so an attic that sounds silent during a daytime visit does not necessarily mean it is inactive. It is simply that the animals are resting.

This pattern holds regardless of the species, which is part of why timing alone tells you that something is active without telling you what it is. Sound weight, movement pattern and location within the attic carry more useful information.

Reading the weight of the sound.

A rat, being larger, tends to produce a heavier, more deliberate scratching or thumping sound, sometimes audible as distinct footsteps if the attic floor is thin plywood over the ceiling below. A mouse produces a lighter, quicker scurrying sound, often described as scratching or scampering rather than footsteps. Neither description is exact for every case, but weight is a genuinely useful first clue when you are trying to decide what you are dealing with before anyone arrives.

Where the sound is coming from matters too.

A sound concentrated in one spot night after night suggests a nest location, while a sound that seems to travel across the ceiling suggests an animal moving along a route rather than resting. Roof rats in particular tend to use consistent paths between an entry point and a nesting area, which can sound like movement crossing from one side of the attic toward another over the course of a few minutes.

On hillside properties near Trousdale Estates and Beverly Park, that travel pattern often connects directly to the canopy and hedge routes described in relation to roof rat behavior generally, since the attic entry point is frequently where an outdoor route meets the structure.

A sound that stays in one spot points to a nest. A sound that moves points to a route.

Other clues beyond sound alone.

Droppings found during a casual attic check, if you have safe access, add useful confirmation. Rat droppings are larger, roughly the size of a grain of rice or bigger, with blunt or spindle-shaped ends depending on species. Mouse droppings are smaller and more granular. A greasy smear along a beam or joist, left behind by repeated body contact along a travel route, is another sign more associated with rats than mice.

Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings if you find them, since disturbing them can release particles into the air. Ventilate the area, avoid direct contact, and mention what you found when you call.

Why guessing wrong delays the actual fix.

Trap placement, station type and the entry points worth checking first all differ somewhat between mice and rats, so an incorrect assumption going into a DIY attempt can waste time on the wrong approach before anyone realizes it was not working. A rodent inspection settles the species question directly, using droppings, gnaw marks, grease trails and the actual entry point found, rather than sound alone.

Once the species is confirmed, attic and crawl space cleanup addresses the nesting material, droppings and any insulation damage left behind, while rat control or mouse control handles the active animals depending on which species the inspection identifies.

How this differs on a Flats bungalow versus a hillside estate.

On a single-level Flats bungalow, attic noise usually sits in one contained space with a single roofline. The most likely candidates are mice working an entry point at the sill or a vent screen, or an occasional roof rat that reached the roofline through a nearby tree. On a multi-level hillside estate, the attic space may span several sections tied to different rooflines. A sound heard in a bedroom ceiling may originate from an entry point on a completely different side of the house, having traveled along the connected attic space in between.

This is one reason a larger property benefits from an inspection that maps the whole attic space rather than only checking near where the noise happens to be heard from below. The noise's apparent location and its actual source are not always the same spot once you account for how far sound and the animal itself can travel within a connected attic.

When noise means more than one animal.

A single mouse or rat produces intermittent noise, often concentrated in short bursts as it moves between resting and foraging. Noise that continues steadily for a longer stretch, or that seems to come from more than one direction at overlapping times, suggests either more than one animal or an active nest with young. Either case points toward calling sooner rather than waiting to see if it resolves on its own. Young animals in a nest are typically quieter than adults but can still be heard as faint, high-pitched sounds if the nest is close to a thin section of ceiling.

Waiting through a growing noise pattern, hoping it resolves itself, tends to allow more chewing, more droppings and a larger cleanup later rather than an easier one. Addressing it while there is a single animal or an early-stage nest is generally more straightforward than addressing it after a colony has fully established itself over several weeks or months.

What to do the night you first hear it.

You do not need to identify the species yourself before calling. Noting when you hear it, roughly where in the attic it seems concentrated, and whether it sounds heavy or light gives us a useful starting point on the call, even if you are unsure which animal it actually is.

Avoid going into the attic at night to investigate directly, since disturbing an active animal in a confined space is not a safe way to confirm anything, and it can simply push the animal to relocate within the attic rather than resolve the situation.

What to note before you call.

  • What time of night you typically hear the noise.
  • Whether it sounds heavy and deliberate or light and quick.
  • Whether the sound stays in one spot or seems to move.
  • Any droppings, gnaw marks or smears you have noticed on a safe, casual look.

Common questions

Could the noise be something other than a rodent?

Birds, squirrels or raccoons can also produce attic noise, generally heavier and more erratic than typical rodent movement. If the pattern does not match mice or rats well, mention that on the call.

Is it normal to hear nothing during the day?

Yes, both mice and rats rest during the day and are typically silent then, so daytime quiet does not mean the attic has no activity.

Can one animal make sounds that seem like more than one?

Yes, a single rat moving between a nest and a food source can produce noise at different times and locations that might otherwise suggest multiple animals.

How fast does attic activity usually get worse if left alone?

It varies by situation, but an unaddressed entry point generally allows continued and sometimes increasing activity over time, since nothing is stopping new animals from following the same route in.

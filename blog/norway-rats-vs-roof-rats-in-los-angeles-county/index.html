Norway Rats vs Roof Rats in Los Angeles County

Two rat species share Beverly Hills and the surrounding cities, and telling them apart changes where you look for entry points.

Two species, two very different habits.

Norway rats and roof rats both live across Los Angeles County, but they use a property very differently. Norway rats are larger, heavier and burrow at ground level, favoring foundations, wood piles and dense ground cover. Roof rats are smaller, lighter and far more agile climbers, favoring trees, hedges and rooflines. Knowing which one you have changes where an inspection should focus first.

How to tell them apart by sight.

A Norway rat has a blunt nose, small ears relative to its head, and a tail noticeably shorter than its body length. A roof rat has a more pointed nose, larger ears, and a tail longer than its body, which helps with balance while climbing. If you only get a brief glimpse, tail length relative to body is usually the easiest single feature to judge.

Droppings offer a second clue when the animal itself is not seen. Norway rat droppings tend to be blunt at both ends and somewhat larger, while roof rat droppings are more spindle-shaped with pointed ends. Neither should be swept or vacuumed dry, since disturbing them can spread particles into the air.

Where each one shows up in Beverly Hills.

Norway rats tend to turn up around foundation gaps, ground-level vents, and burrow holes near hedges or pool equipment pads, more often in Flats-area yards with open ground cover. Roof rats dominate hillside properties near Trousdale Estates and Beverly Park, where mature trees and dense hedges give them a continuous, raised route to the roofline without ever touching the ground.

Both species can appear on the same property in some cases, particularly larger hillside estates with both extensive landscaping and open ground-level areas near a pool or a lower yard. An inspection that only checks one level, only the roofline or only the foundation, can miss a species using the level not checked.

The animal's habits tell you more than its size does.

Why the entry point differs by species.

Norway rats typically enter through foundation gaps, gaps under a slab, torn ground-level vent screens, or burrows dug directly under a wall or a shed. Roof rats typically enter through roofline gaps, torn attic vent screens, chimney flashing seams, or any point where a tree branch or hedge gives direct roof access. Sealing work has to match the species, since closing a roofline gap does nothing for a Norway rat coming in at the foundation, and vice versa.

This is why a rodent inspection checks both levels of a property rather than assuming one species based on the property type alone. A Flats-area yard with mature trees can still see roof rat activity, and a hillside property with an open lower yard can still see Norway rats at ground level.

How treatment differs between the two.

Trap and station placement follows the species' habits. Norway rat placements go along foundation lines, near burrows and at ground-level runways. Roof rat placements go higher, along roofline access points, attic entries and raised runways where the animal actually travels. Rat control covers both species, with placement decided by what the inspection finds rather than a single default approach.

Sealing follows the same logic. Foundation and slab gaps get hardware cloth and mortar at ground level for Norway rats, while roofline vents, flashing and eaves get sealed for roof rats. Rodent exclusion addresses whichever set of gaps the inspection identifies, sometimes both on the same property.

How activity patterns differ by time and season.

Both species are mostly active at night, but roof rats are especially associated with noise heard directly overhead, in an attic or along a roofline, since that is where they spend most of their time. Norway rats more often produce noise at or near ground level, such as scratching behind a low wall, under a shed floor, or along a foundation. If the noise you hear seems to move across a ceiling rather than staying in one wall, that pattern leans toward a roof rat traveling along a route rather than a Norway rat working a fixed burrow.

Seasonally, both species respond to the same dry Southern California summer by relying more heavily on irrigation and landscaping for water. Roof rats shift that reliance toward hillside canopy irrigation lines specifically, while Norway rats shift toward ground-level sprinkler heads, drainage and any low, damp planting bed. As irrigation schedules change in cooler months, both species often move some activity indoors, though roof rats tend to move into attics first while Norway rats tend to move into garages, crawl spaces and basements first.

What this means for outdoor prevention.

Managing ground cover, wood piles and burrow-friendly landscaping helps against Norway rats specifically, while trimming hedges and tree branches back from rooflines helps against roof rats specifically. A property with both risks benefits from both kinds of maintenance rather than assuming one approach covers everything. Backyard and outdoor rodent control addresses ground-level features like burrows and wood piles as part of a broader outdoor plan.

Neither species is inherently worse to have than the other. The practical difference is where they enter and where they travel, which is exactly the information an inspection is built to identify before any trapping or sealing begins. A homeowner who only knows they have a rat problem, without knowing which species, is still in a fine position to call, since sorting that out is part of what the visit itself is for.

Quick species clues.

  • Tail longer than the body points toward a roof rat.
  • Ground-level burrow holes near a foundation point toward a Norway rat.
  • Activity concentrated in an attic or along a roofline points toward a roof rat.
  • Activity near a shed, wood pile or open ground cover points toward a Norway rat.

Common questions

Can Norway rats climb at all?

Yes, but far less readily than roof rats. Norway rats are capable climbers when needed but strongly prefer ground-level movement and burrowing.

Which species is more common in Beverly Hills overall?

Both are present. Hillside properties tend to see more roof rat activity due to canopy access, while flatter yards with open ground cover see relatively more Norway rat activity.

Do the two species compete with each other?

They can, and in some situations one species tends to dominate a given property or area over time, competing for the same food and shelter, though both can still be present simultaneously in different parts of a larger property.

Does the treatment cost differ between the two species?

Cost depends more on the scope of entry points and structures involved than on which species is present, since both require inspection, trapping and sealing tailored to where they actually travel.

Can a single property realistically have both species at once?

Yes, particularly larger properties with both extensive hillside landscaping and open ground-level areas, such as a lower yard near a pool. An inspection that checks both levels is the only reliable way to confirm which are present.
